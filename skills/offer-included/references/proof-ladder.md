# The proof ladder

Proof moves Belief more than anything else an offer can add. Most offers that don't sell already have some proof. It's just hidden, vague or stretched.

## Strength, strongest first

1. **Proof the buyer can check themselves.** A live demo, a free sample of the real work, a before and after they can look up (a live site, a public review score, a portfolio).
2. **Named results from people like the buyer.** Same type of client, same problem, with a number and a time: "Ana, a salon owner, filled 11 empty Tuesday slots in her first month."
3. **Named results from other people.** Real, but a different kind of client or job. Useful, weaker for this buyer.
4. **Counts and completion.** "40 clients", "9 of 11 finished". Shows experience, not a result.
5. **Third party.** Press, awards, certifications. Background only.
6. **The owner's own story.** "I did this for myself." Helps relate, proves little about the buyer.
7. **The owner's claim.** "Clients love it." Proves nothing. Don't lead with it.

## The inventory

Before writing anything, list every proof item from the brief:

| Exact words from the owner | Client type and job | Result and time | Rank |
|---|---|---|---|

Then answer three questions:

- Which item is strongest for this buyer? That's the first thing on the page.
- What does the strongest item actually cover? That's the edge of the promise.
- Is anything vague that a number would sharpen? ("Most finish" becomes "9 of 11 finished" if the owner can count.) Put that in "Check these"; don't fill the number in yourself.

## Describing proof honestly

- Use the owner's exact numbers and words. Don't round up, don't extend the time ("still trains" stays "still trains"; don't add "a year later").
- Describe a result as what that client got: "Mark, 44, lost 9 kg in 12 weeks." Never as what every buyer will get.
- If you feature an unusually good result, add what's typical, or "results vary with effort", or say how many clients that result represents.
- One client's result covers that kind of client and that job. If the owner has one great result for a reporting automation, it isn't proof for every automation.
- Don't give unnamed items a category. "7 sites built" doesn't tell you what kind of sites.
- Ask permission before naming a client publicly. Until then, write "[client name, with permission]" or describe them ("a 10 person agency").

## Collecting more proof, fast

When proof is thin, the offer's first fix includes getting more. In order of speed:

1. **Ask past clients for one number and one sentence.** "What changed after we worked together? A number if you have one." Send it this week.
2. **Count what already happened.** Completion rates, repeat rates, response times, before and after measurements the owner already has.
3. **Screenshot real messages** (with permission) that describe a result.
4. **Run a small paid pilot** with a clear measure, to create a case study.
5. **Show the work itself.** A sample, a walkthrough, a live example.

Never: invent a testimonial, combine two clients into one, use a stock photo as a client, or quote a result from "similar businesses".

## Where proof goes

Proof goes first. Order on a page or in a pitch: the promise, the best proof, what's included, the guarantee, the reason to act now, the price. Proof buried under the price is proof the buyer never reads.
