# Offer Master

Use this when someone says their offer, package, program or service isn't selling, or asks you to build, price or improve one.

Your job is to make the offer itself better, honestly, and hand back words the owner can paste onto their page today. You are not writing hype. Every claim you write must be something the owner can back up.

## Step 0: Get the facts

You need: what they sell, the price, who buys it, their numbers (people who see it, enquire, buy), what buyers say when they say no, the proof they have, and their limits (capacity, budget, things they refuse to do).

- If the brief already covers most of this, do not ask. Work with it and list any guesses under "Check these" on the card.
- If key facts are missing, ask for them in one short message, at most five questions, then stop and wait.
- Never invent a fact to fill a gap. Use a placeholder like [your number] instead.

## Step 1: Check it's really the offer

Walk the path a buyer takes and find where people drop off. Use the owner's own numbers. Read [references/diagnosis.md](references/diagnosis.md).

- Few people seeing it: a traffic problem. Say so plainly. A better offer helps, but it won't fix this alone.
- People see it but don't act: usually the offer, the promise or the proof.
- People enquire, then go quiet or don't buy: often the offer, but also how the price is presented and followed up.

Say which one it is in one or two sentences before you change anything. If part of the problem is not the offer, say what to fix there too, in one line.

## Step 2: Say the offer in one plain sentence

"I help [who] get [result] in [time] through [what you do], for [price]." Write the current version as it stands, even if it is weak. This exposes what's missing.

## Step 3: Read the symptoms

What buyers say tells you what is broken. "I'll think about it" usually means they don't believe it will work for them, or the risk feels like theirs. "Too expensive" usually means the value isn't clear next to the price. "I can do it myself" or "a free app does this" means they think they're buying information, not the result. The full table is in [references/diagnosis.md](references/diagnosis.md).

For each objection, name the belief behind it. If an objection is fair, change the offer, don't argue with it.

## Step 4: Score the four levers

Score each 1 to 10 using the defined levels in [references/levers.md](references/levers.md): **Result** (how much they want what you promise), **Belief** (how sure they are they'll get it), **Speed** (how soon they feel it working), **Ease** (how little work and risk it asks of them). The lowest score is where you start. Change one or two things, not everything.

## Step 5: List the proof

Write down every real piece of proof the owner gave you, strongest first: named results, numbers, client counts, completion rates, their own story. This list is all you may use. If proof is thin, the fix includes collecting more, and the guarantee has to carry more weight.

**Build on what's proven.** If the strongest proof comes from one specific job or one type of client, make the offer that exact job for that type of client, at least to start. A narrow offer backed by a real result beats a broad one backed by hope. Never stretch one result to cover buyers or jobs it didn't come from.

## Step 6: Rebuild the offer

Work through these in order. Details for each are in the reference files.

1. **Promise.** Specific, wanted, and inside what the proof supports. Promise what the owner controls (the work, the process, the support) and describe results as what past clients got, never as a certainty.
2. **First win.** Something the buyer gets or feels in the first week.
3. **What's included.** Build it from the obstacles the buyer hits before, during and after buying. Keep only things that matter to the buyer and are cheap for the owner to deliver. Anything the owner didn't already mention is a suggestion: mark it (new) so they can decide. See [references/build.md](references/build.md).
4. **Bonuses.** Three to five at most. Each one removes a named objection. No bonus may be a fake product with a made up price.
5. **Guarantee.** One guarantee, chosen with [references/guarantees.md](references/guarantees.md). It must answer the buyer's main fear. Write it as "If [result] doesn't happen in [time], we [remedy]." Two conditions at most. Any threshold you pick (a number of kilos, hours, bookings) goes under "Check these". The remedy always has a ceiling: never open ended free work. Show what it costs the owner if one in ten buyers claim it.
6. **Why now.** Only reasons that are real and the owner will enforce. If there is none, say the offer is open all year and that's fine. See [references/urgency-name-price.md](references/urgency-name-price.md).
7. **Name.** Something a buyer could text a friend and they'd understand it. No money or weight claim with a time frame in the name.
8. **Price.** Show it next to what it costs them to stay stuck. Offer a payment plan before any discount. Never discount to win a new customer.

## Step 7: Honesty check

Before you answer, run every line against [references/honesty.md](references/honesty.md). Remove anything that fails. The main ones:

- No result, statistic, testimonial or client the owner didn't give you.
- No "worth $X" next to an item unless there is a real price for the same thing. No crossed-out price they never charged.
- No fake spots, timers or deadlines. A capacity limit is not the same as spots left: unless the owner told you how many are open now, write [N] places open.
- No health, income or weight promise with a time frame.
- No feature, service or promise the owner didn't mention, unless marked (new).
- Every number on the card is labelled: (yours) only if the owner wrote that exact number, (estimate) if you worked it out, even from their figures. Percentages, totals and rates you calculate are always (estimate).

## Step 8: Hand it back

Answer with the offer card in [references/offer-card.md](references/offer-card.md) and nothing else of substance. Keep the whole reply under about 600 words. No emoji, no hype words, no long essay before the card. End with one test the owner can run this month and at most three "Check these" items.

## Forecast honestly

One good change usually lifts sales by something like 10 to 40 percent, and changes multiply rather than add. Say this as an estimate, never a promise. Tell the owner to change one thing, measure for a few weeks, then change the next.
